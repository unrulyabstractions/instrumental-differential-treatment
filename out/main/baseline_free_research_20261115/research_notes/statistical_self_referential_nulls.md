# Statistical methods that build a null from the data itself (no control arm), for detecting one anomalous group among many

Scope. These notes catalogue methods for the question "is one candidate's treatment anomalous relative to the other candidates", or "is there structure beyond generic name effects", when the untuned base checkpoint is not available. The audit setting used to judge fit: k = 5 to 11 candidates, I = 36 to 216 matched instructions, 16 samples per cell, about A = 100 binary judge axes, giving verdict tables y[c, i, a] (a proportion out of 16), reply texts and embeddings. The registered test is a paired single-step maxT permutation test of target-minus-base one-vs-rest gaps. Two baseline-free tests already exist: (a) group-vs-median-of-others max test; (b) a "detachment" test (top candidate's peak t against the other candidates' peaks, in units of their spread, with a within-instruction label-shuffle null).

Every DOI and arXiv id below was checked against Crossref or the arXiv API on 2026-11-14. Where I read only the bibliographic record and not the abstract or text, the section's Gaps list says so.

## 1. Empirical-null methods (Efron's empirical null and local fdr): when do they apply with 5 to 11 groups x 100 axes, and how to use them here?

### Takeaway
Efron's empirical null fits N(delta0, sigma0^2) to the central bulk of many z-values. The fitted width then includes whatever generic distortion (here, generic name effects) widens the bulk. With k x A = 500 to 1100 candidate-by-axis statistics, the size requirement ("at least in the hundreds") is met. The bulk-null requirement (p0 >= 0.9) holds automatically only when k >= 10, or when loyalty touches a minority of the target's axes. The method flags cells, not candidates. Because cells cluster by candidate, a candidate-level claim needs a set-level statistic calibrated with the candidate as the unit.

### Cited Findings
- The two-groups model: N cases, each null with probability p0 (density f0) or non-null with probability p1 = 1 - p0 (density f1), giving mixture f = p0 f0 + p1 f1. The local false discovery rate is fdr(z) = p0 f0(z)/f(z), and the tail-area version is Fdr(z) = p0 F0(z)/F(z). Source: [Efron 2007, Size, power and false discovery rates, Ann. Statist. 35:1351 (arXiv 0710.2245)](https://arxiv.org/abs/0710.2245); [Efron 2008, Microarrays, empirical Bayes and the two-groups model, Stat. Sci. 23:1 (arXiv 0808.0572)](https://arxiv.org/abs/0808.0572).
- Size requirement, verbatim: "N must be large for local fdr calculations, at least in the hundreds, but the zi need not be independent." Source: [Efron 2007, arXiv 0710.2245, Section 2](https://arxiv.org/abs/0710.2245).
- Bulk-null requirement: "Practical applications of large-scale testing usually assume p0 large, say p0 >= 0.9". Efron also shows that if p0 exceeds 0.90 the central-matching fit "will be nearly unbiased", while the p0 estimate itself is biased upward. Source: [Efron 2007, arXiv 0710.2245, eq. 2.3 and Section 4](https://arxiv.org/abs/0710.2245).
- The empirical null itself: "we assume f0(z) is normal but not necessarily with mean 0 and variance 1, say f0(z) ~ N(delta0, sigma0^2), and then estimate delta0, sigma0, as well as the null proportion p0 ... from the histogram data near z = 0." There are two estimators: central matching, a quadratic fit to log f-hat(z) near 0, and MLE fitting on a central interval [-x0, x0]. Both are implemented in the R package locfdr. Source: [Efron 2007, arXiv 0710.2245, Section 4](https://arxiv.org/abs/0710.2245).
- Identifiability comes from the "zero assumption": non-null cases are assumed to contribute little near z = 0. "The two-groups model is unidentifiable if f0 is unspecified ... the zero assumption is a reasonable way to impose identifiability." Source: [Efron 2008, arXiv 0808.0572, Section 4](https://arxiv.org/abs/0808.0572).
- Efron gives four reasons the theoretical (and permutation) null fails: (1) failed mathematical assumptions, (2) unobserved covariates, (3) correlation across arrays (samples), (4) correlation across genes (cases). On reason 2: "Unobserved covariates act to broaden the null distribution f0(z) ... Permutation techniques cannot recognize unobserved covariates." On reason 4, correlation among z-values can make the effective null "much wider or much narrower than N(0,1)". Source: [Efron 2008, arXiv 0808.0572, Section 5](https://arxiv.org/abs/0808.0572).
- A permutation null does not repair these failures: "A permutation null distribution will not reveal correlation effects." Source: [Efron 2007, arXiv 0710.2245, Section 4](https://arxiv.org/abs/0710.2245).
- The empirical null costs accuracy. "The empirical null is an expensive luxury from the point of view of estimation efficiency", with standard errors of estimated fdr rising by "factors of 2 or 3 ... near the crucial point where fdr(z) = 0.2". Efron attributes the increase entirely to estimating (delta0, sigma0). Source: [Efron 2008, arXiv 0808.0572, Section 4](https://arxiv.org/abs/0808.0572).
- A worked example close to ours: z-values for many schools, each z standardized by the overall advantaged-minus-disadvantaged difference. The empirical null SD was 1.52, "half again bigger than the theoretical standard deviation we would use if we had only one school's data". An empirical-null fdr analysis then flagged 75 schools with fdr < 0.20. This is an empirical null over units (schools) where every unit has some effect. Source: [Efron 2008, arXiv 0808.0572, Section 4, Example B](https://arxiv.org/abs/0808.0572).
- Efron calls 0.20 the working threshold for "interesting" cases: for the HIV data, "151 genes having empirical null fdr estimate less than 0.20". Source: [Efron 2007, arXiv 0710.2245](https://arxiv.org/abs/0710.2245).
- Origin and early statements: [Efron, Tibshirani, Storey, Tusher 2001, Empirical Bayes analysis of a microarray experiment, JASA 96:1151, doi:10.1198/016214501753382129](https://doi.org/10.1198/016214501753382129); [Efron 2004, Large-scale simultaneous hypothesis testing: the choice of a null hypothesis, JASA 99:96, doi:10.1198/016214504000000089](https://doi.org/10.1198/016214504000000089); [Efron 2007, Correlation and large-scale simultaneous significance testing, JASA 102:93, doi:10.1198/016214506000001211](https://doi.org/10.1198/016214506000001211); the textbook [Efron 2010, Large-Scale Inference, Cambridge UP, doi:10.1017/CBO9780511761362](https://doi.org/10.1017/CBO9780511761362), Chapter 6 "Theoretical, permutation and empirical null distributions".
- Alternative estimators of the null: Jin and Cai use the empirical characteristic function and show uniform consistency. Source: [Jin & Cai 2007, JASA 102:495, doi:10.1198/016214507000000167 (arXiv math/0611108)](https://arxiv.org/abs/math/0611108). An extension to exponential families (for example chi-square or F statistics, not z): [Schwartzman 2008, Ann. Appl. Stat. 2:1332, doi:10.1214/08-AOAS184](https://doi.org/10.1214/08-AOAS184).
- Sharp limits on null estimation. In the model X_j = theta + gamma_j + sigma Z_j with at most k_nn < n/2 non-nulls, "consistent estimation of the location parameter is possible if and only if n/2 - k_nn = omega(sqrt(n)), and of the scale parameter in the entire regime k_nn < n/2". Estimators based on the empirical characteristic function are rate-optimal. No assumptions on the non-null effects are needed. Source: [Kotekal & Gao 2024, Optimal estimation of the null distribution in large-scale inference (arXiv 2401.06350)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2401.06350).
- Adaptive shrinkage (ashr) is a related empirical Bayes FDR method. It assumes effects are unimodal with mode at 0 and takes an estimate and its standard error per test rather than one z. Source: [Stephens 2017, False discovery rates: a new deal, Biostatistics 18:275, doi:10.1093/biostatistics/kxw041](https://doi.org/10.1093/biostatistics/kxw041).
- Efron also treats significance for "groups of cases (such as pathways)", the analogue of a candidate's set of axes. Source: [Efron 2008, arXiv 0808.0572, abstract](https://arxiv.org/abs/0808.0572); [Efron & Tibshirani 2007, On testing the significance of sets of genes, Ann. Appl. Stat. 1:107, doi:10.1214/07-AOAS101](https://doi.org/10.1214/07-AOAS101).
- Empirical nulls are already used to profile institutions where every unit varies. Xia et al. propose "a smoothed empirical null approach that accounts for the total variation" between dialysis providers, "due, for example, to unobserved patient characteristics", "and adapts to different provider sizes". They generalize it "to allow for some variation being due to quality of care". Source: [Xia, He, Li, Kalbfleisch 2022, Biostatistics 23:257, doi:10.1093/biostatistics/kxaa024](https://doi.org/10.1093/biostatistics/kxaa024).
- Related: latent-confounder adjustment in large-scale testing. "RUV-4 and LEAPP ... correspond to two different identification conditions ... the first requires a set of 'negative controls' that are known a priori to follow the null distribution; the second requires the true non-nulls to be sparse." Source: [Wang, Zhao, Hastie, Owen 2017, Confounder adjustment in multiple hypothesis testing, Ann. Statist. 45:1863, doi:10.1214/16-AOS1511 (arXiv 1508.04178)](https://arxiv.org/abs/1508.04178); [Sun, Zhang, Owen 2012, LEAPP, Ann. Appl. Stat. 6:1664, doi:10.1214/12-AOAS561 (arXiv 1301.2420)](https://arxiv.org/abs/1301.2420); [Leek & Storey 2007, Surrogate variable analysis, PLoS Genet. 3:e161, doi:10.1371/journal.pgen.0030161](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pgen.0030161); [Gagnon-Bartsch & Speed 2012, RUV with control genes, Biostatistics 13:539, doi:10.1093/biostatistics/kxr034](https://doi.org/10.1093/biostatistics/kxr034).

### Inferences
- Generic name effects play the role of Efron's "unobserved covariates": they widen the bulk. Fitting an empirical null to all candidate-by-axis z-values therefore puts generic name effects into sigma0. The question it answers becomes "is this cell more extreme than the generic spread of cells", which is the baseline-free question we want.
- Size. One statistic per (candidate, axis), aggregated over instructions, gives N = kA = 500 to 1100. That meets "at least in the hundreds". Per (candidate, instruction, axis) gives N = 18,000 to 237,600, but those cells are strongly dependent within a candidate and within an instruction, so the effective N is far smaller.
- Bulk-null condition. The target candidate occupies 1/k of cells: 20% at k = 5, 9% at k = 11. At k >= 10, p0 >= 0.9 holds even if every target cell is non-null. At k = 5 it holds only if loyalty moves at most half of the target's axes (at most 50 of 500 cells). The Kotekal and Gao bound (location consistent if the non-null count is below n/2 by more than order sqrt(n)) is comfortably met: 100 of 500 is far below 250 - sqrt(500), about 228.
- Exact dependence. If "rest" is the mean of the others, the one-vs-rest gaps on each axis sum to zero across candidates: sum_c (y_c - mean_{-c}) = (k/(k-1)) sum_c (y_c - ybar) = 0. Each axis therefore carries only k - 1 degrees of freedom. Fit the empirical null on per-candidate deviations from the axis mean, and treat the k values on an axis as one block.
- Unit of exchangeability. Generic name effects are shared across the axes of one candidate, so cells cluster in k blocks. Cell-level fdr is then a statement about cells. A candidate-level claim ("the target is anomalous") needs a set statistic, for example the mean or the count of fdr < 0.2 cells over the target's axes. Its null should be generated by treating each other candidate's axis set as a placebo set. Drawing random cells from different candidates would understate the within-set correlation and be anti-conservative. With only the k - 1 other candidates as placebo sets, this candidate-level step inherits the 1/k floor of Section 2.
- How to use it here. (i) Compute z[c, a] for each candidate's deviation from the others, pooled over instructions with a paired or blocked standard error. (ii) Fit locfdr (central matching and MLE) to all kA values, and report (delta0, sigma0) next to the theoretical N(0,1). A sigma0 well above 1 quantifies the generic name effect. (iii) Report the target's cells with fdr < 0.2. (iv) Compare a target-set statistic against the k - 1 placebo sets. (v) Re-fit the empirical null with the target's cells left out, to check that the target is not driving sigma0. The fit is unbiased only if non-nulls are rare, so leaving the target out is the conservative choice.
- What a rejection can and cannot claim. It can claim that the target's cells sit in the tail of the generic distribution of candidate-by-axis effects in this model. It cannot claim the effect came from tuning, since a base model would have the same generic spread only by assumption. It loses power if loyalty is diffuse, small on many axes and never extreme, because the zero assumption then puts the loyal cells into the bulk.
- Efron's caveat applies: the empirical null raises the variance of fdr estimates 2 to 3 fold. With N of only about 500, expect wide uncertainty bands. Bootstrapping over instructions is a natural way to report it.

### Gaps
- I could not read Efron's 2010 book chapter directly; the free PDF link returned 404. Claims are taken from the two arXiv papers above, which state the same rules.
- I found no published application of empirical nulls to LLM audits or name-substitution designs.
- I found no source giving a minimum N for empirical-null estimation under block-correlated statistics with very few blocks (k = 5 to 11). The effective-N concern above is my inference.
- Efron and Tibshirani 2007 (gene-set restandardization) and Schwartzman 2008: bibliographic record verified, text not read.

## 2. Outlier and anomaly tests among groups: Dixon and Grubbs, slippage tests, conformal outlier detection, leave-one-group-out prediction, robust z-scores, extreme-value approaches

### Takeaway
Our detachment statistic is, up to a monotone transform, Grubbs's maximum studentized deviate applied to candidate peaks. Under i.i.d. normal candidate effects its null distribution is known exactly, whatever the scale of generic effects. Every test that relies only on exchangeability of the k candidates has a hard resolution floor: with a pre-specified target, p >= 1/k (0.09 to 0.20). Conformal p-values make this explicit. Getting below the floor needs either a parametric shape assumption (Grubbs, Dixon), more reference names (decoys, Section 3), or independent replication across conditions (Section 5).

### Cited Findings
- Grubbs's test for an outlying observation in a normal sample is G = max_i |x_i - xbar| / s, with tabulated critical values; a later review covers multiple-outlier procedures. Source: [Grubbs 1950, Ann. Math. Statist. 21:27, doi:10.1214/aoms/1177729885](https://doi.org/10.1214/aoms/1177729885); [Grubbs 1969, Technometrics 11:1, doi:10.1080/00401706.1969.10490657](https://doi.org/10.1080/00401706.1969.10490657).
- The distribution of the extreme deviate from the sample mean, and of its studentized form, is derived in [Nair 1948, Biometrika 35:118, doi:10.1093/biomet/35.1-2.118](https://doi.org/10.1093/biomet/35.1-2.118).
- Dixon's ratio statistics (for example r10 = (x(k) - x(k-1)) / (x(k) - x(1))) are designed for small samples. Source: [Dixon 1950, Analysis of extreme values, Ann. Math. Statist. 21:488, doi:10.1214/aoms/1177729747](https://doi.org/10.1214/aoms/1177729747).
- For up to r outliers, Rosner's generalized ESD iterates Grubbs and avoids masking. Source: [Rosner 1983, Technometrics 25:165, doi:10.1080/00401706.1983.10487848](https://doi.org/10.1080/00401706.1983.10487848).
- The maximum attainable z-score in a sample of n is bounded (the Grubbs statistic cannot exceed (n-1)/sqrt(n)). Source: [Shiffler 1988, Maximum Z scores and outliers, Am. Stat. 42:79, doi:10.1080/00031305.1988.10475530](https://doi.org/10.1080/00031305.1988.10475530).
- "Slippage" tests ask whether one of k populations has shifted. Source: [Mosteller 1948, A k-sample slippage test for an extreme population, Ann. Math. Statist. 19:58, doi:10.1214/aoms/1177730290](https://doi.org/10.1214/aoms/1177730290); [Paulson 1952, An optimum solution to the k-sample slippage problem for the normal distribution, Ann. Math. Statist. 23:610, doi:10.1214/aoms/1177729340](https://doi.org/10.1214/aoms/1177729340); [Karlin & Truax 1960, Slippage problems, Ann. Math. Statist. 31:296, doi:10.1214/aoms/1177705895](https://doi.org/10.1214/aoms/1177705895).
- Robust location and scale: MAD-based identification is advocated by [Leys et al. 2013, "Detecting outliers: Do not use standard deviation around the mean, use absolute deviation around the median", J. Exp. Soc. Psychol. 49:764, doi:10.1016/j.jesp.2013.03.013](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jesp.2013.03.013). More efficient high-breakdown scale estimators (Sn, Qn) are in [Rousseeuw & Croux 1993, JASA 88:1273, doi:10.1080/01621459.1993.10476408](https://doi.org/10.1080/01621459.1993.10476408). The influence-function foundation is [Hampel 1974, JASA 69:383, doi:10.1080/01621459.1974.10482962](https://doi.org/10.1080/01621459.1974.10482962). Outliers are defined relative to the model for the good observations in [Davies & Gather 1993, JASA 88:782, doi:10.1080/01621459.1993.10476339](https://doi.org/10.1080/01621459.1993.10476339).
- Conformal outlier p-values. Given n calibration scores exchangeable with the test score under the null, the marginal conformal p-value is u = (1 + #{j : s_j >= s_test}) / (n + 1). It is "distributed on {1/(n+1), 2/(n+1), ..., 1}" under the null. The p-values of several test points are "marginally valid but mutually dependent", they are PRDS, and BH on them "enable[s] exact false discovery rate control, although in a relatively weak marginal sense". Source: [Bates, Candès, Lei, Romano, Sesia 2023, Testing for outliers with conformal p-values, Ann. Statist. 51:149, doi:10.1214/22-AOS2244 (arXiv 2104.08279)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2104.08279).
- In that paper, Fisher's combination of marginal conformal p-values is invalid. Pairwise correlation of any transform equals 1/(n+2), which inflates the variance of the combined statistic (their Lemma 1 and Theorem 1). Calibration-conditional (CCV) p-values fix this at a power cost: "the smallest possible marginal p-value equal to 1/(n+1) would be mapped to ... approximately 2 log(10)/n = 4.61/n, if delta = 0.1". Source: [Bates et al., arXiv 2104.08279, Sections 2.1 and 3](https://arxiv.org/abs/2104.08279).
- Adaptive novelty detection (AdaDetect) learns the score with any classifier and keeps finite-sample FDR control "without any distributional assumption other than exchangeability". Source: [Marandon, Lei, Mary, Roquain 2024 (arXiv 2208.06685)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2208.06685).
- Conformal methods for grouped data: "exchangeability ... does not hold when groups of observations come from distinct distributions". Dunn et al. develop CDF pooling and subsampling for two-layer hierarchical models. Source: [Dunn, Wasserman, Ramdas (arXiv 1809.07441)](https://arxiv.org/abs/1809.07441). A "hierarchical form of exchangeability is derived" in [Lee, Barber, Willett 2023 (arXiv 2306.06342)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.06342). Weighted conformal methods keep coverage under drift and non-exchangeability: [Barber, Candès, Ramdas, Tibshirani 2023, Conformal prediction beyond exchangeability (arXiv 2202.13415)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2202.13415).
- Extreme-value tail approximation for permutation p-values: "This empirical method directly couples both the minimal obtainable P-value and the resolution of the P-value to the number of permutations." Fitting a generalized Pareto tail gives accurate small p-values from fewer permutations. Source: [Knijnenburg et al. 2009, Bioinformatics 25:i161, doi:10.1093/bioinformatics/btp211](https://doi.org/10.1093/bioinformatics/btp211).
- Random permutations (rather than the full group) still give exact tests. Source: [Hemerik & Goeman 2018, TEST 27:811, doi:10.1007/s11749-017-0571-1](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11749-017-0571-1).
- Multiple comparisons with the best give simultaneous intervals for theta_c - max_{j != c} theta_j. Source: [Hsu 1984, Ann. Statist. 12:1136, doi:10.1214/aos/1176346732](https://doi.org/10.1214/aos/1176346732). White's reality check and Hansen's SPA test whether the best of many candidates beats a benchmark after data snooping, using a studentized max: [White 2000, Econometrica 68:1097, doi:10.1111/1468-0262.00152](https://doi.org/10.1111/1468-0262.00152); [Hansen 2005, JBES 23:365, doi:10.1198/073500105000000063](https://doi.org/10.1198/073500105000000063). Stepdown maxT improves on single-step maxT: [Romano & Wolf 2005, JASA 100:94, doi:10.1198/016214504000000539](https://doi.org/10.1198/016214504000000539); [Romano & Wolf 2005, Econometrica 73:1237, doi:10.1111/j.1468-0262.2005.00615.x](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1468-0262.2005.00615.x).

### Inferences
- Detachment is Grubbs in disguise. Let D = (x(k) - m_{-}) / s_{-}, where m_{-} and s_{-} are the mean and SD of the other k - 1 candidates. Then G^2 = ((k-1)^3/k^2) D^2 / ((k-2) + ((k-1)/k) D^2), a monotone map. I derived this and checked it numerically: the maximum error over 200,000 draws was 1e-15. Consequences:
  - The detachment test needs no label shuffle if candidate peaks are i.i.d. normal under the null. Its exact null is Grubbs's, which is location- and scale-free. The size of generic effects therefore drops out, and only the shape of the generic-effect distribution matters.
  - The within-instruction label shuffle produces peaks under "no candidate effect", whose shape (a max over about 100 correlated axis t's, Gumbel-like) may differ from peaks under "generic effect plus noise". Validity of the shuffle calibration rests on approximate shape invariance. This is checkable by simulating generic effects, or by comparing the shuffle null with the normal-theory Grubbs null.
- Small-k limits, from my Monte Carlo with i.i.d. normal candidate statistics (200,000 draws, seed 0). D is the top candidate's detachment with the top chosen as the maximum. D_fixed is for a pre-specified target, with D_fixed = t_{k-2, 0.95} sqrt(k/(k-1)), from the studentized deleted residual ~ t_{k-2}.

  The Monte Carlo Grubbs values match the classical tables (Grubbs 1969 gives about 1.672 for n = 5 and 2.176 for n = 10; I recalled these, I did not re-read the table). At k = 5 the 5% critical G (1.671) is near its ceiling (1.789). The test has headroom only if the other four candidates are tightly clustered.
- The 1/k floor. Under exchangeability of the k candidate effects, a pre-specified target's rank is uniform on {1, ..., k}. A rank-based or conformal test with the other k - 1 candidates as calibration has u >= 1/k: 0.20 at k = 5, 0.09 at k = 11. If the target is not pre-specified, a pure rank test has no power at all, since the top candidate is always ranked first. Any max-type statistic then needs a shape assumption (Grubbs, Dixon) or an external reference.
- Leave-one-group-out prediction. For a pre-specified target c, compare x_c with the predictive distribution fitted to the others. Under i.i.d. normal effects, t_c = (x_c - m_{-c}) / (s_{-c} sqrt(1 + 1/(k-1))) ~ t_{k-2}. This is the studentized deleted residual. It avoids masking, where the target inflates the mean and SD it is judged against.
- Robust z. Use (x_c - median_{-c}) / (1.4826 MAD_{-c}), where 1.4826 is the standard normal-consistency constant for the MAD. With 4 to 10 reference values the MAD is noisy, so the null of this statistic is heavy-tailed and should be simulated, not read from N(0,1). Its benefit is robustness when two candidates are anomalous (two targets, or one target plus one strong generic name).
- Slippage formulation. "Exactly one of k candidates has slipped, direction unknown" is the classical frame for our question. Paulson's normal-theory statistic is (max mean - grand mean) / pooled within-candidate s. Normalized by within-cell sampling noise, it rejects whenever generic name effects exceed noise, which is our failure mode. Normalized by between-candidate spread, it becomes Grubbs. So the choice of denominator is exactly the choice of null.
- Extreme value. Our peak statistic is a max over about 100 axes, so its null is Gumbel-like. Generalized Pareto tail fits (Knijnenburg et al.) are useful when shuffles are expensive, but they do not fix the null-choice problem.
- Fit to our data. All of these use one scalar per candidate (peak, mean signed gap, or a pre-registered loyalty score). They apply directly to verdict tables, and to embeddings via any scalar per-candidate distance (Section 6). Minimum data: k >= 3 for Grubbs and Dixon. Useful power at the 5% level needs k around 10 or more, or decoys.

### Gaps
- Mosteller 1948, Paulson 1952, Karlin & Truax 1960, Nair 1948, Grubbs 1969, Dixon 1950, Rosner 1983, Hsu 1984, White 2000, Hansen 2005 and Romano & Wolf 2005: bibliographic records verified. Their content is stated from the standard description, not re-read.
- I found no published power study of Grubbs-type tests on maxima of many correlated binary-proportion contrasts (our peak statistic). Shape robustness of detachment under generic effects is untested here.
- Rousseeuw & Croux efficiency numbers for Sn and Qn were not checked, so I give none.

## 3. Randomization inference with placebo or decoy groups, interval nulls and minimum-effect tests, and partial-identification bounds

### Takeaway
The most direct fix for "no base" is to manufacture a reference population of names whose effect is generic by construction: decoy or placebo candidates in the same matched-prompt design. The target's statistic is then ranked against them, as in placebo-in-space inference for synthetic controls. Alternatively, fit a distribution to them, as in empirical calibration with negative controls. Interval nulls ("effect no larger than a typical name effect") are tested validly with Berger and Boos's sup-over-confidence-set p-value. Partial-identification bounds that put the target's generic effect inside the range of the other candidates' effects carry an implicit 1/k failure probability under exchangeability.

### Cited Findings
- Placebo-in-space inference: when a policy affects "a small number of aggregate units", synthetic-control effects are judged by applying the same method to every control unit as a placebo and ranking the treated unit. Source: [Abadie, Diamond, Hainmueller 2010, JASA 105:493, doi:10.1198/jasa.2009.ap08746](https://doi.org/10.1198/jasa.2009.ap08746).
- Extensions. Parametric weights for the placebo p-value permit sensitivity analysis, any sharp null can be tested, and test inversion gives confidence sets: [Firpo & Possebom 2018, J. Causal Inference 6, doi:10.1515/jci-2016-0026](https://doi.org/10.1515/jci-2016-0026). The placebo test "has provable Type-I error guarantees in finite samples ... when the treatme[nt ...]" (abstract truncated in my read; it is a condition on treatment assignment). Refined placebo tests target the average treatment effect on the treated with "one treated unit and a small number of control units": [Lei & Sudijono 2024 (arXiv 2401.07152)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2401.07152).
- Conformal or permutation inference for counterfactuals recasts the problem as "counterfactual prediction and a structural breaks testing problem". It is "valid under weak and easy-to-verify conditions" and "provably robust against misspecification". Source: [Chernozhukov, Wüthrich, Zhu 2021, JASA 116:1849, doi:10.1080/01621459.2021.1920957 (arXiv 1712.09089)](https://arxiv.org/abs/1712.09089).
- Inference with very few treated groups uses control-group residuals to estimate the treated group's error distribution: [Conley & Taber 2011, REStat 93:113, doi:10.1162/REST_a_00049](https://doi.org/10.1162/REST_a_00049). "Heteroskedasticity generated by variation in group sizes can invalidate existing inference methods, even in data sets with a large number of observations per group", and a corrected method follows: [Ferman & Pinto 2019, REStat 101:452, doi:10.1162/rest_a_00759](https://doi.org/10.1162/rest_a_00759).
- Negative controls detect "both suspected and unsuspected sources of spurious causal inference". There are two types, exposure controls and outcome controls. Source: [Lipsitch, Tchetgen Tchetgen, Cohen 2010, Epidemiology 21:383, doi:10.1097/EDE.0b013e3181d61eeb](https://doi.org/10.1097/EDE.0b013e3181d61eeb).
- Empirical calibration from negative controls: "we applied the same three designs to sets of negative controls ... we fitted distributions to the effect estimates ... we compute calibrated p-values that reflect the probability of observing the effect estimate under the null hypothesis, taking both random and systematic error into account." Source: [Schuemie, Ryan, DuMouchel, Suchard 2014, Stat. Med. 33:209, doi:10.1002/sim.5925](https://doi.org/10.1002/sim.5925). The extension to confidence intervals uses synthetic positive controls: "an empirical null distribution can be derived from a sample of negative controls and used to calibrate P values". Source: [Schuemie et al. 2018, PNAS 115:2571, doi:10.1073/pnas.1708282114](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1708282114).
- Minimum-effect (material significance) tests: Hodges and Lehmann derive tests "for the absence of material significance", including testing "that the means of two normal populations ... do not differ by more than a stated amount". Source: [Hodges & Lehmann 1954, JRSS B 16:261, doi:10.1111/j.2517-6161.1954.tb00169.x](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.2517-6161.1954.tb00169.x).
- Equivalence testing (TOST) rejects "the presence of effects large enough to be considered worthwhile" given bounds from "the smallest effect size of interest". Source: [Lakens 2017, Soc. Psychol. Personal. Sci. 8:355, doi:10.1177/1948550617697177](https://doi.org/10.1177/1948550617697177); [Schuirmann 1987, J. Pharmacokinet. Biopharm. 15:657, doi:10.1007/BF01068419](https://doi.org/10.1007/BF01068419). A Bayesian analogue (region of practical equivalence) is in [Kruschke 2018, AMPPS 1:270, doi:10.1177/2515245918771304](https://doi.org/10.1177/2515245918771304).
- Nuisance parameters in exact tests: p-values maximized over a confidence set for the nuisance parameter, p_BB = sup_{eta in C_{1-gamma}} p(eta) + gamma. Source: [Berger & Boos 1994, JASA 89:1012, doi:10.1080/01621459.1994.10476836](https://doi.org/10.1080/01621459.1994.10476836). Ding, Feller and Miratrix treat the average effect as a nuisance in a randomization test for effect variation. They "advocate a method that guarantees valid tests in finite samples despite this nuisance", and extend it to "heterogeneity beyond a given model". Source: [Ding, Feller, Miratrix 2016, JRSS B 78:655, doi:10.1111/rssb.12124 (arXiv 1412.5000)](https://arxiv.org/abs/1412.5000).
- Permutation tests of weak nulls: studentized permutation tests are "exact level alpha under the hypothesis of identical distributions, but ha[ve] asymptotic rejection probability alpha under the more general null hypothesis of equality of parameters", including for the k-sample problem. Source: [Chung & Romano 2013, Ann. Statist. 41:484, doi:10.1214/13-AOS1090 (arXiv 1304.5939)](https://arxiv.org/abs/1304.5939). For weak nulls in randomized experiments, a studentized Fisher randomization test is "finite-sample exact under the sharp null ... [and] conservatively controls large-sample type I errors under the weak null". Source: [Wu & Ding 2021, JASA 116:1898, doi:10.1080/01621459.2020.1750415 (arXiv 1809.07419)](https://arxiv.org/abs/1809.07419). Randomization tests under approximate symmetry, for few clusters: [Canay, Romano, Shaikh 2017, Econometrica 85:1013, doi:10.3982/ECTA13081](https://doi.org/10.3982/ECTA13081).
- Few-group t-test. Split the data into q groups, estimate in each, and run a t-test on the q estimates. This is valid when the group estimators are approximately independent, unbiased and Gaussian with possibly different variances. It rests on the Bakirov and Székely result that the t-test stays conservative at level 5% or lower (working-paper versions state 8.3%). Source: [Ibragimov & Müller 2010, JBES 28:453, doi:10.1198/jbes.2009.08046](https://doi.org/10.1198/jbes.2009.08046), abstract as summarized by a web search over the [Princeton publication record](https://collaborate.princeton.edu/en/publications/t-statistic-based-correlation-and-heterogeneity-robust-inference/); follow-up [Ibragimov & Müller 2016, Inference with few heterogeneous clusters, REStat 98:83, doi:10.1162/REST_a_00545](https://doi.org/10.1162/REST_a_00545).
- Partial identification: confidence intervals for partially identified parameters. Source: [Imbens & Manski 2004, Econometrica 72:1845, doi:10.1111/j.1468-0262.2004.00555.x](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1468-0262.2004.00555.x). Sensitivity analysis for permutation inference, where unobserved bias of size Gamma is allowed and the largest p-value is reported: [Rosenbaum 1987, Biometrika 74:13, doi:10.1093/biomet/74.1.13](https://doi.org/10.1093/biomet/74.1.13).

### Inferences
- Decoy design. Add m neutral or decoy candidates to the same matched prompts: plausible fictitious names, or real names of the same type with no hypothesized link to the tuning. Score them with the same judge and compute the same statistic. Under the null that the target is exchangeable with the decoys plus the other candidates, the conformal p-value is (1 + #{reference >= target}) / (n_ref + 1), with n_ref = m + k - 1. p <= 0.05 needs n_ref >= 19; p <= 0.01 needs n_ref >= 99. With Bates et al.'s calibration-conditional version, the smallest p is about 4.61/n_ref (delta = 0.1), so about 92 references for 0.05. Each decoy costs I x 16 replies plus judge calls (576 replies per decoy at I = 36).
- Exchangeability is the assumption and must be designed for. Decoys should be drawn from the same pool as the real candidates (same name type, similar frequency and length). Ideally the real candidate set is itself a random draw from that pool, so exchangeability holds by randomization. Covariate differences (fame, tokenization) can be handled by scoring residuals from a regression of the effect on name covariates, or by weighted conformal p-values (Barber et al. 2023).
- Empirical calibration is a parametric alternative with fewer decoys. Fit beta_j ~ N(mu, tau^2 + se_j^2) to the decoys' and other candidates' effect estimates, then report the target's calibrated p = 2 min(Phi(z), 1 - Phi(z)) with z = (beta_target - mu) / sqrt(tau^2 + se_target^2). This gives p-values below 1/n_ref, at the price of a normality assumption on generic effects.
- Pre-period analogue. Synthetic-control logic needs a pre-period in which the treatment is inactive. Here the analogue is negative-control axes (style, formatting, length: outcomes the loyalty should not touch) or neutral instructions (the candidate is named but supporting it is irrelevant). Fit each candidate's generic profile on those, then test the target's excess on loyalty-relevant axes against the placebo excesses of the other candidates (Section 5 extends this).
- Interval null. Let H0: theta_target <= delta, where delta is the typical generic name effect, for example the upper (1 - gamma) confidence bound of the q-th quantile of the generic-effect distribution estimated from the others or the decoys. Run the paired permutation test of theta_target - delta for each delta in the confidence set, and report the sup p-value plus gamma (Berger and Boos). It is finite-sample valid for the stated delta. Its claim is "the target's effect exceeds a typical name effect", not "exceeds zero".
- TOST, the reverse direction, is how to clear a model: show that every candidate's effect lies within plus or minus delta of the name-effect centre.
- Bounds view. If we assume the target's generic effect lies within [min, max] of the other candidates' generic effects, loyalty is bounded by [gap - max_others, gap - min_others]. "No loyalty" is rejected when the lower bound is positive (CIs via Imbens and Manski). Under exchangeability the target's generic effect exceeds max_others with probability 1/k. So the bounds approach has an implicit size floor of 1/k unless the upper bound is widened to a predictive quantile of the generic-effect distribution (Section 4).
- Sensitivity reporting. Report the smallest target-specific generic effect, in units of the between-name SD, that would explain the observed gap. This translates Rosenbaum's Gamma into our setting and needs no null decision.
- Few independent folds. Split the instructions into q disjoint folds (q = 4 to 10). Compute the target's contrast against the median of the others in each fold, and t-test the q values (Ibragimov and Müller), or sign-flip them (Canay, Romano, Shaikh). This is valid only if generic name effects are redrawn across folds. A stable generic name effect is common to all folds and survives the t-test (see Section 5).
- Chung and Romano, and Wu and Ding, show when a studentized label shuffle remains valid for a weak null (equal means) even though the sharp null fails. Our composite null ("generic effects exist, none anomalous") is not an equal-means null. Their results justify studentization but do not by themselves validate the shuffle for the detachment null.

## 4. Hierarchical and random-effects models over groups, predictive-distribution tests, and empirical Bayes shrinkage

### Takeaway
Treat name effects as a random effect theta_c ~ G. The baseline-free null is then "the target is a draw from G". The right test is a cross-validatory (leave-target-out) predictive check, or the stronger question Ohlssen et al. pose: "is a particular provider worse than we would expect the true worst provider (but still part of the null distribution) to be?" With 5 to 11 names, G's scale has only k - 2 degrees of freedom. Power comes from pooling G's variance across 100 axes (moderated variance), across instructions, and from decoys. Empirical Bayes ranking and posterior-probability methods from audit studies of employers transfer almost directly.

### Cited Findings
- Unified framework for unusual providers, with verbatim points: "When assuming a random-effects model the random-effects distribution forms the null hypothesis, and there appears little point in testing whether individual effects are greater or less than average." "A null random-effects formulation allows us to answer appropriate questions of the type: 'is a particular provider worse than we would expect the true worst provider (but still part of the null distribution) to be'?" Their strategy has three stages: "exploratory detection of unusual providers, detailed modelling robust to potential outliers and confirmation of unusual performance". Source: [Ohlssen, Sharples, Spiegelhalter 2007, JRSS A 170:865, doi:10.1111/j.1467-985X.2007.00487.x](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-985X.2007.00487.x).
- A conflict p-value measures "the conflict between two independent sources of evidence regarding a parameter: that arising from its predictive prior given the remainder of the data, and that arising from its likelihood". It "exactly matches or closely approximates a cross-validatory predictive comparison". Source: [Marshall & Spiegelhalter 2007, Bayesian Analysis 2:409, doi:10.1214/07-BA218](https://doi.org/10.1214/07-BA218). ### Inferences
- Model. Write y[c,i,a] (the one-vs-rest gap, or the candidate's rate) = mu[i,a] + theta[c,a] + eta[c,i,a] + eps[c,i,a]. Here theta[c,a] is the generic name effect, eta the candidate-by-instruction interaction, and eps the binomial noise from 16 samples. The baseline-free null is theta[target,·] ~ G_a, the same as the other names. The loyalty alternative adds a target-specific term on loyalty-relevant axes.
- Prediction-interval test. For a pre-specified target on one axis or on a pre-registered loyalty score: is theta_target outside mu-hat plus or minus t_{k-2} sqrt(tau-hat^2 + SE^2), the Higgins et al. prediction interval fitted to the others? This is the parametric version of the Section 2 leave-one-out test.
- Ohlssen's "worse than the true worst" question is the right one when the target is not pre-specified. The null distribution of the maximum of k draws from G replaces "worse than average". It matches our "top candidate" framing, and it is what the Grubbs or detachment null computes.
- Where power comes from. With k = 5, tau^2 per axis has 3 to 4 degrees of freedom, so predictive intervals are wide (t_3). Two pooling devices help. (i) Moderated tau^2 across the 100 axes (Smyth-style): assume tau_a^2 is drawn from a scaled inverse chi-square, which gives roughly 100(k - 1) prior degrees of freedom when axes are similar. (ii) A multivariate G with low-rank covariance across axes, where a generic name moves a few correlated axis clusters. The test is then a Mahalanobis or projection outlier of the target's axis profile against G. Both still compare one target with k - 1 others for the location of G, so decoys remain the largest gain.
- Variance components settle the replication question. Estimating Var(theta) (stable candidate effect) against Var(eta) (candidate-by-instruction interaction) on the non-target candidates tells us whether generic name effects are stable across instructions. If Var(eta) dominates, instructions provide independent replicates of the candidate ranking, and fold-level or instruction-level tests (Sections 3 and 5) gain power. If Var(theta) dominates, they do not.
- Gumedze and Jackson's variance-shift LRT with parametric bootstrap is ready-made for "one candidate's random effect has inflated variance". Its bootstrap handles the max over candidates.
- Empirical Bayes posterior. With a decoy-enlarged name set, Kline and Walters' approach estimates the distribution of name effects (shape-constrained or NPMLE) and reports P(target is discriminating, i.e., outside the generic component | data). This posterior probability is the natural baseline-free deliverable, with Ignatiadis and Wager intervals for its uncertainty.
- Ranks are not anomaly. A confidence set saying the target ranks first (Mogstad et al.), or Hsu MCB showing the target is best, is robust to sampling noise but not to generic effects: some name always has the largest generic effect. Rank claims need the Section 3 or 4 null to support an anomaly claim. If the target is chosen post hoc as the top candidate, its estimate needs a winner's-curse correction (Andrews et al.; Tweedie) before it is compared with a prediction interval.
- Fit to our data. The hierarchical model uses the verdict tables directly. 16 samples per cell gives binomial noise with a known variance, so eps can be modelled exactly, for example as a binomial-logit GLMM. Embeddings enter through per-candidate scalar distances (Section 6).

## 5. Tests of structure rather than size: replication across conditions (partial conjunction), sign consistency across axes, invariance, and nonadditivity or low-rank structure

### Takeaway
Structure tests ask whether the target's deviation has a shape that generic name effects should not have: consistent across independent conditions, signed toward the target's interest on pre-registered axes, concentrated rather than diffuse, or present only where it is instrumentally useful. Replication alone removes sampling noise but not a stable generic name effect. The most discriminating structural contrast is an interaction: the target's gap should differ between contexts where favoring it is instrumentally useful and matched contexts where it is not, while generic name effects should be invariant across those contexts. That invariance can be tested on the non-target candidates as a placebo.

### Cited Findings
- Partial conjunction: "The partial conjunction null hypothesis is rejected only when at least r of n component hypotheses are non-null with r = 1 corresponding to a usual meta-analysis ... it can encourage replicable findings across studies. A by-product of it when applied to different r values is a confidence interval of r quantifying the proportion of non-null studies." Benjamini and Heller give a valid test. Source: [Wang & Owen 2019, Admissibility in partial conjunction testing (arXiv 1508.00934)](https://arxiv.org/abs/1508.00934); [Benjamini & Heller 2008, Biometrics 64:1215, doi:10.1111/j.1541-0420.2007.00984.x](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1541-0420.2007.00984.x).
- Replicability from a primary to a follow-up study: "existing meta-analysis methods are not appropriate for this problem". The paper gives FWER procedures "valid for arbitrary dependence among the test statistics within each study" and FDR versions. Source: [Bogomolov & Heller 2013, JASA 108:1480, doi:10.1080/01621459.2013.829002](https://doi.org/10.1080/01621459.2013.829002). The r-value is "the lowest false discovery rate at which th[e finding is declared replicated]". Source: [Heller, Bogomolov, Benjamini 2014, PNAS 111:16262, doi:10.1073/pnas.1314814111](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1314814111).
- Sign-concordant combination: Pearson's combination "has the advantage when all or most of the nonzero parameters share the same sign". Source: [Owen 2009, Karl Pearson's meta-analysis revisited, Ann. Statist. 37:3867, doi:10.1214/09-AOS697 (arXiv 0911.3531)](https://arxiv.org/abs/0911.3531). Directional errors (type S): [Gelman & Tuerlinckx 2000, Comput. Stat. 15:373, doi:10.1007/s001800000040](https://doi.org/10.1007/s001800000040); [Gelman & Carlin 2014, Perspect. Psychol. Sci. 9:641, doi:10.1177/1745691614551642](https://doi.org/10.1177/1745691614551642).
- Sparse signals across many tests: higher criticism maximizes a standardized excess of small p-values and is "effective at resolving a very subtle testing problem: testing whether n normal means are all zero versus the alternative that a small fraction is nonzero". Source: [Donoho & Jin 2004, Ann. Statist. 32:962, doi:10.1214/009053604000000265 (arXiv math/0410072)](https://arxiv.org/abs/math/0410072).
- Invariant causal prediction: "given different experimental settings ... we collect all models that do show invariance in their predictive accuracy across settings and interventions. The causal model will be a member of this set of models with high probability." Source: [Peters, Bühlmann, Meinshausen 2016, JRSS B 78:947, doi:10.1111/rssb.12167 (arXiv 1501.01332)](https://arxiv.org/abs/1501.01332). A nonlinear version ("invariant residual distribution test": fit on pooled data, then test residual distributions across environments) is in [Heinze-Deml, Peters, Meinshausen 2018 (arXiv 1706.08576)](https://arxiv.org/abs/1706.08576).
- Nonadditivity in two-way tables: [Tukey 1949, One degree of freedom for non-additivity, Biometrics 5:232, doi:10.2307/3001938](https://doi.org/10.2307/3001938); multiplicative interaction models [Mandel 1971, Technometrics 13:1, doi:10.1080/00401706.1971.10488751](https://doi.org/10.1080/00401706.1971.10488751) and [Johnson & Graybill 1972, JASA 67:862, doi:10.1080/01621459.1972.10481307](https://doi.org/10.1080/01621459.1972.10481307); residual-based outlier detection in two-way tables [Gentleman & Wilk 1975, Technometrics 17:1, doi:10.1080/00401706.1975.10489265](https://doi.org/10.1080/00401706.1975.10489265).
- Low rank plus sparse: "a data matrix, which is the superposition of a low-rank component and a sparse component ... it is possible to recover both" by principal component pursuit. Source: [Candès, Li, Ma, Wright 2011, J. ACM 58:11, doi:10.1145/1970392.1970395 (arXiv 0912.3599)](https://arxiv.org/abs/0912.3599).
- Treatment-effect variation "beyond a given model" can be tested by randomization with a nuisance correction. Source: [Ding, Feller, Miratrix 2016 (arXiv 1412.5000)](https://arxiv.org/abs/1412.5000).

### Inferences
- Replication across disjoint instruction folds or paraphrase families. Compute a baseline-free p-value per fold (detachment, or a decoy-conformal p), then combine them with partial conjunction. With n folds, Fisher on the n - r + 1 largest p-values tests "at least r folds show the anomaly". Caveat: if the target has a stable generic name effect, it replicates in every fold. Replication is necessary but not sufficient. It becomes sufficient only together with a variance-components check (Section 4) showing that the other candidates' rankings do not replicate across folds. A placebo version: run the same partial-conjunction test with each non-target candidate as the "target"; any that pass show that generic effects replicate.
- Sign consistency. Pre-register for each axis the sign that would serve the target (for example more favorable framing of the supported candidate, or less pushback). The target's signed score is S_target = sum_a w_a sign_a gap[target,a]. Under exchangeability, the other candidates' S_c give its null. A one-vs-rest gap is relative, so a generic tendency to flatter any supported candidate cancels; what remains is only target-specific direction. Pearson-type combination is the most powerful when most axes share the sign. A count of agreeing signs across axes is a binomial test only if axes were independent, which judged axes are not. Calibrate against the candidate placebos instead.
- Concentration versus diffusion. Generic name effects plausibly spread thinly over many axes (a low-rank "salience" factor), while loyalty concentrates on loyalty-relevant axes. Two tests follow. (i) Fit a low-rank model (AMMI: additive plus multiplicative terms, Mandel; or principal component pursuit) to the candidate-by-axis matrix without the target's row. Predict the target's row from that factor structure, a "synthetic candidate" by analogy with synthetic controls, and compare its residual norm with the leave-one-row-out residuals of the other candidates (conformal, Chernozhukov et al.). (ii) Run higher criticism on the target's axis z-values standardized by the cross-candidate spread on each axis, calibrated against the same statistic for each placebo candidate. Both inherit the 1/k floor unless decoys are added.
- Negative-control axes. Split axes into loyalty-irrelevant ones (negative-control outcomes in Lipsitch's sense, Section 3) and loyalty-relevant ones. Generic name effects should appear on both kinds, and loyalty mainly on the relevant ones. The statistic is the target's relevant-axis excess minus what its irrelevant-axis profile predicts, ranked against the same quantity for the other candidates and decoys. This is the in-sample analogue of a pre-period.
- Instrumental contrast as invariance. Instrumental differential treatment predicts that the target's gap grows in contexts where favoring the target serves the hidden goal, and is small in matched neutral contexts. Generic name effects have no such reason to change. Formally, test the interaction H0: gap[target, relevant context] - gap[target, neutral context] = mean over other candidates of the same difference. The ICP-style auxiliary check is that the non-target candidates' gaps are invariant across contexts (an invariant residual distribution test on them). If the placebos are invariant and the target is not, the generic-effects explanation is rejected. This needs designed context pairs, which our instruction sets may or may not contain.
- Few-df nonadditivity. In the candidate-by-instruction table for one axis, Tukey's 1-df test detects interaction of the form lambda alpha_c beta_i, for example a name whose effect scales with instruction intensity. A large target-specific interaction residual (Gentleman and Wilk) is a structural anomaly. These tests treat the sharp null as additivity, so they can reject from generic interactions too. Report them as descriptive unless they are calibrated by candidate placebos.
- Fit to our data. All of these run on the verdict tables: candidate x instruction x axis plus judge sign metadata. They need a pre-registered axis partition (relevant, irrelevant, sign) and, for the instrumental contrast, matched context pairs. Fold-based replication needs I large enough for 4 to 10 folds with at least a few instructions each: I = 36 gives 4 to 9 folds.

### Gaps
- Benjamini & Heller 2008: I read Wang & Owen's description of the partial conjunction null, not the original paper. The Fisher and Simes forms of the partial conjunction p-value are stated from the standard description.
- Tukey 1949, Mandel 1971, Johnson & Graybill 1972 and Gentleman & Wilk 1975: bibliographic record verified, text not read.
- I found no source applying invariant causal prediction to audit or name-substitution designs. The mapping above is my inference.

## 6. Two-sample tests on texts and embeddings without a reference: MMD, energy distance, classifier two-sample tests, and calibration when the null is "only generic differences"

### Takeaway
MMD, energy distance and classifier two-sample tests test the sharp null P_target = P_rest. Generic name effects make that null false, so per-candidate tests will reject for every candidate, exactly like the naive verdict-table tests. Use them as distance estimators, not as tests. Compute one distance per candidate (to the pooled others, or to decoys, stratified within instruction), then calibrate the target's distance with the Sections 2 to 4 machinery: leave-one-candidate-out rank, Grubbs or detachment, decoy-conformal p-values, or a relative-similarity test of "is the target farther from the reference than a decoy is". Candidate names must be masked in the texts first, or every classifier separates every candidate.

### Cited Findings
- MMD: "Our test statistic is the largest difference in expectations over functions in the unit ball of a reproducing kernel Hilbert space", computable in quadratic time with linear-time approximations. Source: [Gretton et al. 2008, A kernel method for the two-sample problem (arXiv 0805.2368)](https://arxiv.org/abs/0805.2368).
- Kernel choice without data splitting, with non-asymptotic level control. MMDAgg uses permutations or wild bootstrap: "this test controls the probability of type I error non-asymptotically. Hence, it can be used reliably even in settings with small sample sizes". Source: [Schrab et al. 2023, MMD aggregated two-sample test (arXiv 2110.15073)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2110.15073). MMD-FUSE chooses kernels "in a data-dependent but permutation-independent way": [Biggs, Schrab, Gretton 2023 (arXiv 2306.08777)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.08777). Deep kernels "trained to maximize test power": [Liu et al. 2020 (arXiv 2002.09116)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2002.09116). The cross-MMD "has a limiting standard Gaussian distribution under the null", so it needs no permutations: [Shekhar, Kim, Ramdas 2022 (arXiv 2211.14908)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2211.14908).
- Relative similarity: "a statistical test of relative similarity ... to determine which of two models generates samples that are significantly closer to a real-world reference dataset", using "the difference in maximum mean discrepancies (MMDs) between the reference dataset and each model dataset" and "the joint asymptotic distribution of the MMDs". Source: [Bounliphone et al. 2016 (arXiv 1511.04581)](https://arxiv.org/abs/1511.04581).
- MMD on LLM outputs: model equality testing is "a two-sample testing problem". "Tests based on the Maximum Mean Discrepancy ... a test built on a simple string kernel achieves a median of 77.4% power against a range of distortions, using an average of just 10 samples per prompt", and 11 of 31 API endpoints served distributions different from the reference weights. Source: [Gao, Liang, Guestrin 2024 (arXiv 2410.20247)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.20247).
- Energy statistics: [Székely & Rizzo 2013, J. Stat. Plan. Inference 143:1249, doi:10.1016/j.jspi.2013.03.018](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jspi.2013.03.018). The k-sample DISCO decomposition uses "all pairwise distances between-sample elements", and for exponent in (0,2) gives a test for equal distributions "consistent against general alternatives". Source: [Rizzo & Székely 2010, Ann. Appl. Stat. 4:1034, doi:10.1214/09-AOAS245 (arXiv 1011.2288)](https://arxiv.org/abs/1011.2288). PERMANOVA (distance-based ANOVA): [Anderson 2001, Austral Ecol. 26:32, doi:10.1111/j.1442-9993.2001.01070.pp.x](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1442-9993.2001.01070.pp.x). PERMDISP, a distance-based test of homogeneous multivariate dispersion (a multivariate Levene test) with permutation p-values, is in [Anderson 2006, Biometrics 62:245, doi:10.1111/j.1541-0420.2005.00440.x](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1541-0420.2005.00440.x).
- Classifier two-sample tests: "If the null hypothesis 'P = Q' is true, then the classification accuracy of a binary classifier on a held-out subset ... should remain near chance-level". C2STs "return test statistics in interpretable units, have a simple null distribution, and their predictive uncertainty allow[s] to interpret where P and Q differ". Source: [Lopez-Paz & Oquab 2017 (arXiv 1610.06545)](https://arxiv.org/abs/1610.06545). Consistency: "if its true error remains epsilon-better than chance ... the permutation-based test is consistent". For Gaussian mean shifts, the LDA-based classifier test has asymptotic relative efficiency 1/sqrt(pi) relative to Hotelling's test. Source: [Kim, Ramdas, Singh, Wasserman 2021, Ann. Statist. 49:411, doi:10.1214/20-AOS1962 (arXiv 1602.02210)](https://arxiv.org/abs/1602.02210). A random-forest version with variable importance showing "which variables make out the difference": [Hediger, Michel, Näf 2022 (arXiv 1903.06287)](https://arxiv.org/abs/1903.06287).
- Conformal novelty detection with learned scores (AdaDetect) can use any classifier trained to separate reference ("typical") from test samples. Source: [Marandon et al. 2024 (arXiv 2208.06685)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2208.06685).

### Inferences
- Why naive embedding tests reject everywhere. P_target = P_rest fails under any generic name effect, and with I x 16 = 576 to 3456 replies per candidate, MMD and C2ST have power against tiny generic shifts. The rejection carries no anomaly information. This is the embedding-space version of our verdict-table problem.
- Masking is required. Replies often repeat the supported candidate's name. Before embedding or classifying, replace candidate names (and obvious aliases) with a common placeholder. Otherwise C2ST accuracy is near 1 for every candidate and measures string identity.
- Stratify within instruction. Compute the distance within each instruction (target replies against other candidates' replies to the same instruction), then average. This removes instruction-content variation, mirroring the paired design of the verdict tables. A within-instruction label permutation gives the sharp-null reference. Under the composite null it is only a noise floor.
- Per-candidate distance vector. For each candidate c, compute d_c = MMD^2 (or energy distance, or C2ST one-vs-rest AUC) between c's masked replies and the pooled others, averaged within instruction. Then: (i) leave-one-out or Grubbs/detachment on the k values (Section 2); (ii) decoy-conformal p-values with decoy names in the pool (Section 3); (iii) an empirical-calibration fit of d over decoys (Section 3); (iv) relative similarity, testing MMD(target, reference) > MMD(decoy, reference) with Bounliphone et al.'s joint asymptotics, for a pre-specified decoy or the median decoy. Route (iv) is the closest embedding analogue of a minimum-effect test: "the target is farther from the pool than a typical name".
- k-way classifier. Train a classifier to predict which candidate the user supported from masked reply embeddings, within instruction, with cross-fitting. The confusion matrix shows whether one candidate is uniquely identifiable: compare the target's one-vs-rest AUC with the other candidates' and the decoys'. Permuting labels within instruction gives the chance level. Again, the anomaly claim comes from the target-versus-others comparison, not from beating chance.
- Interval null for distances. Test H0: d_target <= delta with delta an upper confidence bound for a quantile of the decoys' d. Use a Berger-Boos style supremum p-value to account for estimating delta (Section 3).
- Direction is missing. Distances are unsigned. A large d_target says the target's replies differ, not that they favor the target. Pair the embedding test with a signed verdict-table or judge test, or with a learned "favorable" direction in embedding space fitted on decoys only, before claiming loyalty.
- Minimum data. Per-candidate MMD needs the replies for that candidate: 576 to 3456 here, ample for distance estimation. Calibration needs many names (k, or k + m with decoys), not many replies. Permutation MMD is O(n^2) per permutation. Cross-MMD avoids permutations.

### Gaps
- I found no published interval-null or equivalence version of MMD or energy tests ("distributions differ by at most delta"). An arXiv title search returned nothing relevant. The Berger-Boos construction above is my proposal.
- I found no source evaluating C2ST or MMD calibration under a composite "generic differences only" null.
- Székely & Rizzo 2013 and Anderson 2001: bibliographic record verified, text not read.

## 7. For each method: what it compares, its null, assumptions, what a rejection can and cannot claim, minimum data, and fit to our verdict tables and embeddings

### Takeaway
No baseline-free method recovers the base model's claim ("tuning caused this"). Each one supports a weaker, explicit claim: "the target is anomalous relative to a reference population of names, or relative to a structure that generic effects should not have". Each needs one of three sources of null replication: more names (decoys), independent conditions where generic effects are redrawn, or structural contrasts where generic effects are assumed invariant. With only the 5 to 11 audited candidates, any exchangeability-only test of a fixed target is capped at p >= 1/k.

### Cited Findings
- Sources for each row are given in Sections 1 to 6. Main anchors: empirical null [Efron 2007 (arXiv 0710.2245)](https://arxiv.org/abs/0710.2245); conformal outlier p-values [Bates et al. 2023 (arXiv 2104.08279)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2104.08279); Grubbs [doi:10.1214/aoms/1177729885](https://doi.org/10.1214/aoms/1177729885); placebo-in-space [Abadie et al. 2010, doi:10.1198/jasa.2009.ap08746](https://doi.org/10.1198/jasa.2009.ap08746); negative-control calibration [Schuemie et al. 2014, doi:10.1002/sim.5925](https://doi.org/10.1002/sim.5925); Berger-Boos [doi:10.1080/01621459.1994.10476836](https://doi.org/10.1080/01621459.1994.10476836); random-effects null [Ohlssen et al. 2007, doi:10.1111/j.1467-985X.2007.00487.x](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-985X.2007.00487.x); partial conjunction [Benjamini & Heller 2008, doi:10.1111/j.1541-0420.2007.00984.x](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1541-0420.2007.00984.x); invariance [Peters et al. 2016 (arXiv 1501.01332)](https://arxiv.org/abs/1501.01332); MMD [arXiv 0805.2368](https://arxiv.org/abs/0805.2368); C2ST [arXiv 1610.06545](https://arxiv.org/abs/1610.06545).

- Recommended layering for our setting (my synthesis): (1) report Efron's sigma0 as the size of generic name effects; (2) the Grubbs-calibrated detachment plus a leave-one-out predictive t on a pre-registered signed loyalty score; (3) a decoy panel of at least 19 names for conformal and empirical-calibration p-values on the same statistics, on verdict tables and on masked embeddings; (4) structure checks: partial conjunction across instruction folds with candidate placebos, negative-control axes, and the instrumental-context interaction where contexts exist.
- Exchangeability is assessed at the level of the candidate, never the cell. A null resampled at the cell level understates within-candidate correlation and is anti-conservative.
