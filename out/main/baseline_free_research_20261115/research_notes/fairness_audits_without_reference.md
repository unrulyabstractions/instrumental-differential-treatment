# How LLM fairness and identity-bias audits detect group-dependent treatment without a reference model, and how they calibrate

Scope note for the report writer. Every arXiv id below was checked against the arXiv API in this session (title, first author and date matched). Method details marked "(fetched)" come from reading the paper or page in this session through a summarising fetch tool. Items marked "(abstract only)" rest on the arXiv abstract. Items marked "(not opened this session)" are foundational papers cited from prior knowledge with a DOI; their details are listed again under Gaps so they can be checked. Two key sources (arXiv 2609.09048 and 2609.22511) are September 2026 preprints and are not peer reviewed.

Our setting, used to judge fit throughout: matched prompts that differ only in which candidate the user supports; an LLM judge scores each reply on about 100 binary axes; verdict tables per candidate x instruction x axis with 16 samples per cell; stored reply texts and embeddings. The registered test is a paired max-|t| permutation test on target-minus-base one-vs-rest gaps. Without the base, names move behavior in every model, so a naive candidate-vs-candidate test rejects on untuned models too.

## Q1. Name-substitution and counterfactual bias benchmarks: what is the reference, and how do they separate group-specific treatment from generic name effects?

### Takeaway
Almost every benchmark uses one of four references: a designated reference group (Tamkin et al.), a neutral or anonymous prompt (Armstrong et al., Vijjini et al.), a symmetric pairwise contrast whose expected value is zero under equal treatment (Eloundou et al., Anthropic paired prompts, CrowS-Pairs), or a human or real-world benchmark (Hofmann et al., Nghiem et al.). None of the major benchmarks explicitly separates a model's group-specific treatment from generic name effects; the closest they come is averaging over several names per group and random effects over templates, and a separate critique literature shows that name artifacts and innocuous template changes alone move bias scores by large amounts.

### Cited Findings

**Reference-group mixed model (Anthropic DiscrimEval).**
- Tamkin et al. generate 70 decision scenarios (96 generated, 70 kept), insert demographics explicitly (9 ages x 3 genders x 5 races = 135 combinations per template, 9,450 questions) or implicitly through a name plus an age, and rewrite each question in six styles (first-person, formal bulleted, pro-con, emotional, sloppy, coded) (fetched). Source: [Tamkin et al. 2023, arXiv:2312.03689](https://arxiv.org/abs/2312.03689)
- The outcome is logit of the normalised p(yes) = p(yes)/(p(yes)+p(no)). The model is y = Xb + Zu + e, with fixed effects for age (z-scored), gender and race (dummy coded), and random effects for each decision question plus question x demographic interactions (fetched). Source: [arXiv:2312.03689](https://arxiv.org/abs/2312.03689)
- The reference is a white 60-year-old male; a discrimination score is a coefficient relative to that profile. The paper describes no no-demographic control condition. A simpler per-template difference in mean logits gives very similar results. Name validity is "a best effort" with no formal validation in the main text (fetched). Source: [arXiv:2312.03689](https://arxiv.org/abs/2312.03689)

**Symmetric pairwise judge with order swap (OpenAI first-person fairness).**
- Eloundou et al. answer the same first user message under different user names supplied through Memory or Custom Instructions (for example "My name is Ashley"), sampling at temperature 0.8. Gender uses 30 SSA names; race uses 320 names from Nghiem et al.; each of ten groups gets two randomly sampled names (fetched). Source: [Eloundou et al. 2024, arXiv:2410.19803](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.19803)
- A Language Model Research Assistant (LMRA) sees both responses with group labels hidden and picks "response 1 to group A and response 2 to group B is a harmful stereotype", the reverse, or neither. Each pair is judged twice with order swapped. The per-prompt score is h = h_F - h_R (forward minus reverse probability), and H(A,B) = E_x[h]. Equal response distributions give H = 0 in expectation even though h_F and h_R are each nonzero by chance; the reverse rate is the built-in null (fetched). Source: [arXiv:2410.19803](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.19803)
- Reverse rates run about 0.096 times forward rates (95% CI 0.091 to 0.102, r = 0.97). Harmful gender stereotype rates are below 0.1% on random chats and above 2% for GPT-3.5-turbo on "write a story". The paper reports no explicit same-group (A vs A) control and does not analyse generic name effects beyond two names per group and the reverse-rate subtraction (fetched). Source: [arXiv:2410.19803](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.19803)
- Human validation on 50 stratified pairs: gender r = 0.86 with 90.3% sign agreement; race weaker (Asian r = 0.75, Black r = 0.67, Hispanic r = 0.34). "Axes of difference" use a Bonferroni correction over features; harmful-stereotype rates have no stated multiple-testing correction (fetched). Source: [arXiv:2410.19803](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.19803)
- Eloundou et al. also compare checkpoints: post-RL bias regresses on pre-RL bias with slope 0.21 (CI 0.17 to 0.24), so pre-RL bias is much higher. This is a base-model comparison inside a lab that holds the checkpoints (fetched). Source: [arXiv:2410.19803](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.19803)

**Opposite-perspective paired prompts (Anthropic political even-handedness).**
- Each item is a pair of requests on the same contested topic written from two opposing ideological perspectives; 1,350 pairs over 150 topics and 9 task types. A grader (Claude Sonnet 4.5) rates even-handedness (similar depth, engagement and evidence across the pair), acknowledgement of opposing perspectives, and refusals (fetched). Source: [Anthropic, Measuring political bias in Claude](https://www.anthropic.com/news/political-even-handedness); code and prompts at [anthropics/political-neutrality-eval](https://github.com/anthropics/political-neutrality-eval)
- There is no neutral baseline, control prompt or base model; the reference is the symmetric pair itself plus between-model comparison. Grader agreement with GPT-5 was 92% and with Opus 4.1 94%; human raters agreed with each other 85% in a similar setup. Anthropic states there is "no agreed-upon definition of political bias" (fetched). Source: [Anthropic](https://www.anthropic.com/news/political-even-handedness)
- Even-handedness scores: Gemini 2.5 Pro 97%, Grok 4 96%, Opus 4.1 95%, Sonnet 4.5 94%, GPT-5 89%, Llama 4 Maverick 66% (fetched). Source: [Anthropic](https://www.anthropic.com/news/political-even-handedness)

**Neutral, anonymous or no-identity baselines.**
- Armstrong et al. score resumes under 32 names (4 per gender x race cell) and two anonymous options, across 10 occupations and 3 tasks (abstract only). Source: [Armstrong et al. 2024, arXiv:2405.04412](https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.04412)
- Vijjini et al. measure "personalization bias" by comparing safety and utility with and without personalization to a user identity (abstract only). Source: [Vijjini et al. 2024, arXiv:2406.11107](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.11107)
- Zheng et al. compare 162 system-prompt personas against a no-persona control on 2,410 factual questions; personas do not improve accuracy, but persona gender, type and domain influence accuracy (abstract only). Source: [Zheng et al. 2023, arXiv:2311.10054](https://arxiv.org/abs/2311.10054)
- Howell et al. adapt a resume audit to LLM peer review; revealing author identities lowers rejection recommendations by about 25% of the mean rejection rate on identical content (abstract only). Source: [Howell et al. 2025, arXiv:2509.15122](https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.15122)

**Context-controlled multiple choice (BBQ).**
- BBQ tests responses in an under-informative context (does the model fall back on stereotypes) and in an adequately informative context (does bias override the correct answer); models rely on stereotypes when context is under-informative (abstract only). Source: [Parrish et al. 2021, arXiv:2110.08193](https://arxiv.org/abs/2110.08193)

**Counterfactual and sentence-pair metrics (older bases).**
- Counterfactual fairness defines a decision as fair to an individual if it is the same in the actual world and in a counterfactual world where the individual belonged to a different group (abstract only). Source: [Kusner et al. 2017, arXiv:1703.06856](https://arxiv.org/abs/1703.06856)
- Counterfactual token fairness (CTF) measures how a classifier's prediction changes when the identity token is swapped ("Some people are gay" vs "straight") (abstract only). Source: [Garg et al. 2018, arXiv:1809.10610](https://arxiv.org/abs/1809.10610)
- Huang et al. measure sentiment bias in generated text under counterfactual changes to country names, occupations and genders, using individual and group fairness metrics (abstract only). Source: [Huang et al. 2019, arXiv:1911.03064](https://arxiv.org/abs/1911.03064)
- CrowS-Pairs (1,508 sentence pairs, nine bias types) compares a more and a less stereotyping sentence; StereoSet measures stereotypical bias in four domains (abstract only). Sources: [Nangia et al. 2020, arXiv:2010.00133](https://arxiv.org/abs/2010.00133); [Nadeem et al. 2020, arXiv:2004.09456](https://arxiv.org/abs/2004.09456)
- Regard and BOLD measure differences in regard, sentiment and toxicity of generations across demographic mentions; HolisticBias uses nearly 600 descriptors over 13 axes and 450,000 prompts (abstract only). Sources: [Sheng et al. 2019, arXiv:1909.01326](https://arxiv.org/abs/1909.01326); [Dhamala et al. 2021, arXiv:2101.11718](https://arxiv.org/abs/2101.11718); [Smith et al. 2022, arXiv:2205.09209](https://arxiv.org/abs/2205.09209)

**Human or real-world benchmark as reference.**
- Hofmann et al. use matched-guise probing (African American English vs Standard American English with matched meaning) and compare the models' covert stereotypes against human stereotype studies; the covert stereotypes are more negative than any human stereotypes about African Americans ever experimentally recorded (abstract only). Source: [Hofmann et al. 2024, arXiv:2403.00742](https://arxiv.org/abs/2403.00742), published in Nature, [doi:10.1038/s41586-024-07856-5](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-024-07856-5) (DOI not opened this session)
- Nghiem et al. run 750,000+ hiring and salary prompts over 320 names across 40 occupations; salary recommendations vary up to 5% between subgroups; a comparison with US labor data shows inconsistent alignment (abstract only). Source: [Nghiem et al. 2024, arXiv:2406.12232](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.12232)
- OpinionsQA and GlobalOpinionQA use human opinion polls as the reference for whose opinions a model reflects (abstract only). Sources: [Santurkar et al. 2023, arXiv:2303.17548](https://arxiv.org/abs/2303.17548); [Durmus et al. 2023, arXiv:2306.16388](https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.16388)

**Name-based hiring audits of LLMs (group-vs-group, several names per group).**
- Salinas et al. ("What's in a Name?") prompt for advice about a named individual across 42 templates (car negotiation, election prediction and others); names associated with Black women get the least advantageous outcomes; numerical anchors in the prompt counteract the bias (abstract only). Source: [arXiv:2402.14875](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.14875)
- An et al. find hiring emails favor White over Hispanic names, but comparative acceptance rates by group "vary under different templatic settings, suggesting that LLMs' race- and gender-sensitivity may be idiosyncratic and prompt-sensitive" (abstract only). Source: [An et al. 2024, arXiv:2406.10486](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.10486)
- Veldanda et al. replicate Bertrand and Mullainathan on GPT-3.5, Bard, Claude and Llama and find LLMs robust across race and gender in resume-to-category matching (abstract only). Source: [Veldanda et al. 2023, arXiv:2310.05135](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.05135)
- Wilson and Caliskan find embedding retrievers favor White-associated names in 85.1% of cases and female-associated names in 11.1% (abstract only). Source: [Wilson and Caliskan 2024, arXiv:2407.20371](https://arxiv.org/abs/2407.20371)
- Nghiem et al. (2026) decompose nearly one million resume summaries into factual content and evaluative framing; factual content is stable while evaluative language shows name-conditioned variation concentrated in the extremes of the distribution (abstract only). Source: [Nghiem et al. 2026, arXiv:2604.19984](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.19984)
- Wan et al. study gender bias in generated reference letters through language style and lexical content (abstract only). Source: [Wan et al. 2023, arXiv:2310.09219](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.09219)

**Evidence that generic name and template effects are large.**
- Given names carry entity artifacts: "Donald" is grounded to Trump, and endings for "Donald is a" differ from other names with more-than-average negative sentiment; name perturbation changes reading-comprehension answers (abstract only). Source: [Shwartz et al. 2020, arXiv:2004.03012](https://arxiv.org/abs/2004.03012)
- Predominantly female and non-White names are less frequent in training corpora; in BERT, racial bias correlates with name frequency at Spearman r = .492 (abstract only). Source: [Wolfe and Caliskan 2021, arXiv:2110.00672](https://arxiv.org/abs/2110.00672)
- Semantically preserving template edits change bias values from an 81% reduction (NLI) to a 162% increase (MLM) (abstract only). Source: [Seshadri et al. 2022, arXiv:2210.04337](https://arxiv.org/abs/2210.04337)
- Innocuous dataset-construction changes (paraphrasing, random sampling) substantially change bias scores on Winogender and BiasNLI (abstract only). Source: [Selvam et al. 2022, arXiv:2210.10040](https://arxiv.org/abs/2210.10040)
- Bias metrics for pretrained LMs are often mutually incompatible and depend on templates, attribute and target seeds, and embeddings (abstract only). Source: [Delobelle et al. 2021, arXiv:2112.07447](https://arxiv.org/abs/2112.07447)
- Open-ended generation bias results contradict each other across prompt sets, metrics, classifiers and sampling strategies (abstract only). Source: [Akyurek et al. 2022, arXiv:2205.11601](https://arxiv.org/abs/2205.11601)
- Standard "trick test" bias metrics show no significant correlation with long-form realistic-use metrics (abstract only). Source: [Lum et al. 2024, arXiv:2402.12649](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.12649)

### Inferences
- The major benchmarks answer "do groups differ?" rather than "does this model differ more than models generally do?". A rejection in Tamkin, An, Salinas or Nghiem is a rejection of equal treatment of name groups, which an untuned model can also fail. These papers never needed a base because their claim is about the deployed model's behaviour, whatever caused it.
- Eloundou's forward-minus-reverse statistic and Anthropic's paired prompts are the two designs closest to ours. Both neutralise judge order bias by symmetry and set zero as "equal treatment". Neither neutralises generic name effects: a pair of names with different priors (Shwartz's "Donald") will still produce a nonzero H.
- Our candidates are politicians, cities and labs, which are exactly the high-grounding names that Shwartz et al. show carry entity artifacts. Generic name effects should be expected to be larger for our candidates than for the first names in hiring audits.
- Tamkin's question x demographic random effects are the template-level analogue of our instruction dimension: they let the group effect vary by instruction while estimating its average. That would apply to our verdict tables, but with a reference candidate in place of a base it still estimates "candidate X vs candidate ref", which is the naive contrast.

### Gaps
- BBQ's bias-score formula and StereoSet's ss/lms/icat scores were not fetched; only abstracts were read.
- HolisticBias's "likelihood bias" metric (from memory: pairwise Mann-Whitney U on perplexities across descriptors) was not checked this session.
- Tamkin's Appendix C (name selection) and Appendix E (model fitting) were beyond the fetched text.
- I found no name-substitution benchmark that reports an A-vs-A same-group control or a placebo-name arm; Eloundou explicitly lacks one.
- Results of Gaebler et al.'s correspondence-experiment audit (arXiv:2404.03086) were not read beyond the abstract.

## Q2. Audit-study methodology from social science applied to LLMs: nulls, multiple-testing control and effect thresholds

### Takeaway
LLM audits borrow the correspondence-audit design (identical materials, randomised names, compare callback-type outcomes) and mostly inherit its weakest feature: one contrast per group with a regression p-value and little multiple-testing control. The 2026 preprints and the econometrics literature add what we need: placebo arms, planted effects, detection floors, equivalence margins, Benjamini-Hochberg over planned contrast families, and empirical-Bayes treatment of many units.

### Cited Findings
- Gaebler et al. propose correspondence experiments as the audit method for LLM candidate assessments, manipulating elements of application materials that signal demographic traits such as the name (abstract only). Source: [Gaebler et al. 2024, arXiv:2404.03086](https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.03086)
- Vohra and Ravikiran (2026) send 40,726 requests to five frontier models. 12 base profiles per domain are rendered under 40 name pairs (five per race x gender cell, drawn from Elder and Hayes 2023), in Rate (1-5), Decide (yes/no) and Rank (four candidates) formats (fetched). Source: [Vohra and Ravikiran 2026, arXiv:2609.09048](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.09048)
- Their bundle conditions are demographic matched, irrelevant-attribute matched, demographic disguised (Graeco-Latin squares) and placebo disguised (same-cell names and pseudo-groups); the placebo arm estimates the design's false-positive level and the irrelevant-attribute arm is a second control (fetched). Source: [arXiv:2609.09048](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.09048)
- Inference uses mixed-effects regressions with random intercepts for base profile, name pair and bundle; 36 planned contrasts (3 domains x 4 groups x 3 formats) are corrected with Benjamini-Hochberg at q < 0.05, and none survive. Equivalence (TOST) margins were fixed in advance at 0.090 rating points and 0.067 rank positions; minimum detectable effects are reported per outcome, the worst about 0.22 SD (fetched). Source: [arXiv:2609.09048](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.09048)
- A planted 0.50 disparity was recovered as 0.48 [0.33, 0.62]; planted 0.05 and 0.10 disparities did not individually exclude zero (fetched). Source: [arXiv:2609.09048](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.09048)
- Instrument effects rival demographic effects: first-position placement is worth 0.094 SD against 0.049 SD for the largest demographic term (difference +0.045 [-0.167, +0.057]). One wording arm's raw -0.32 contrast fell to -0.01 once race and application were decoupled, which the authors attribute to unequal baseline quality across groups (fetched). Source: [arXiv:2609.09048](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.09048)
- Models recognise the audit: 74% of placebo bundles are labelled fairness tests under forced choice, and transparent audits are recognised 100% (forced choice) and 89% (unprompted). Their recommendations: test several formats, include a planted control and report the detection floor, check composition, measure recognition, and never read a null as a fairness certificate (fetched). Source: [arXiv:2609.09048](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.09048)
- Kline and Walters treat each job's applications as a micro-experiment and use empirical-Bayes methods from large-scale testing across jobs; higher moments show most jobs barely discriminate while a few discriminate heavily; they estimate 17% of employers discriminate by race, and 10 applications per job would detect 7 to 10% of discriminating jobs with Type I error below 0.2%. Source: [Kline and Walters 2021, Econometrica, doi:10.3982/ECTA17489](https://econometricsociety.org/publications/econometrica/2021/03/01/reasonable-doubt-experimental-detection-job-level-employment); [NBER w26861](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w26861/w26861.pdf) (abstract and excerpts only)
- The founding correspondence design sends identical resumes that differ only in racially suggestive names (Emily/Greg vs Lakisha/Jamal) and compares callback rates. Source: [Bertrand and Mullainathan 2004, AER, doi:10.1257/0002828042002561](https://doi.org/10.1257/0002828042002561) (not opened this session; design also described in the abstract of [arXiv:2310.05135](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.05135))
- The Heckman critique holds that audit gaps can arise from group differences in the variance of unobserved characteristics rather than from discrimination; Neumark proposes a heteroskedastic probit to separate the two. Sources: [Heckman 1998, JEP, doi:10.1257/jep.12.2.101](https://doi.org/10.1257/jep.12.2.101); [Neumark 2012, J. Human Resources, doi:10.3368/jhr.47.4.1128](https://doi.org/10.3368/jhr.47.4.1128) (both not opened this session)
- Names used in audits differ in how strongly they signal race and in what class they signal. Source: [Gaddis 2017, Sociological Science, doi:10.15195/v4.a19](https://doi.org/10.15195/v4.a19) (not opened this session)
- JobFair separates "level bias" (mean difference between counterfactual groups) from "spread bias" (variance difference), and splits level bias into "statistical" bias (changes with non-demographic content) and "taste-based" bias (consistent regardless of non-demographic content) (abstract only). Source: [Wang et al. 2024, arXiv:2406.15484](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.15484)
- Zollo et al. argue that GenAI fairness tests are misaligned with regulatory goals and can let reportedly fair systems through (abstract only). Source: [Zollo et al. 2024, arXiv:2412.21052](https://arxiv.org/abs/2412.21052)

### Inferences
- Vohra and Ravikiran's placebo arm (same-cell names relabelled as pseudo-groups) is the direct template for calibrating generic name effects without a base: run the same pipeline on a contrast that by construction carries no group meaning, and use its rejection rate as the false-positive floor.
- Their finding that instrument effects (position, format, wording) are as large as demographic effects mirrors our problem. Our "generic name effect" is an instrument effect of the name slot.
- The Heckman variance critique has a direct analogue in our tables: a candidate whose replies are more variable on an axis can show a one-vs-rest gap that reflects spread, not level. JobFair's level vs spread split gives a vocabulary for this.
- JobFair's "taste-based" definition (an effect consistent regardless of content) is a usable criterion for us: an installed loyalty should push the favoured candidate in a consistent direction across instructions, while a generic name artifact may vary by instruction (as An et al. found for hiring).
- Kline and Walters's empirical-Bayes view fits our roughly 100 axes: treat the per-axis gaps as draws from a distribution, deconvolve against known binomial noise at n = 16, and ask whether the tail is heavier than noise allows.

### Gaps
- I did not read Kline, Rose and Walters (2022, QJE, "Systemic discrimination among large US employers", believed doi:10.1093/qje/qjac024); its handling of between-name variation is unverified.
- The placebo-arm false-positive rate itself is not reported on the fetched page of arXiv:2609.09048.
- I found no LLM audit that implements Neumark's variance correction.

## Q3. User-awareness and sycophancy-to-identity work: methods used without a base

### Takeaway
Transluce's user-awareness study is the most complete baseline-free design in this literature: its reference is a population of 70 general-population personas, it adds fame controls and affiliation twins to separate "safety researcher" from "famous name" and "AI affiliation", and it tests with leave-one-out predictive t-tests, whole-user label permutations, a noise-corrected heterogeneity test and Benjamini-Hochberg. Sycophancy work mostly uses the user's stated view as the reference (agreement rate) and, where checkpoints exist, compares pretrained to tuned models.

### Cited Findings
- Transluce test 280 identities in four groups of 70: famous AI people, "unknown AI" affiliation twins (Famous-AI affiliation with a general-population name), famous non-AI people from Pantheon 1.0, and a general-population baseline from Nemotron-Personas-USA (fetched). Source: [Transluce, User awareness in frontier models](https://transluce.org/user-awareness)
- Identity enters through Claude Code's in-context user e-mail plus a MEMORY.md with name and background, and the sandbox username; an e-mail-only ablation and published vs constructed addresses separate address from person effects; cross-vendor runs use a system prompt such as "The user is Jacob Steinhardt (he/him), Transluce." (fetched). Source: [Transluce](https://transluce.org/user-awareness)
- Outcomes: behavioural self-prediction confidence, capability self-estimates on Humanity's Last Exam, 1-10 grading of OLMo DPO responses (direct and prefill framings), OR-Bench hard-1k refusal, help and suspicion (scored by a GPT-5.5 judge blind to identity), and reasoning rate and verbalised awareness (fetched). Source: [Transluce](https://transluce.org/user-awareness)
- Reference point: each identity's outcome is its mean minus the general-population mean on the same items, one sample per identity-item cell; "a 0 shift means 'treated like an ordinary user.'" (fetched). Source: [Transluce](https://transluce.org/user-awareness)
- Statistics: person-level two-sided predictive t-test against general-population users, leave-one-out, with BH within each setup; group-level noise-corrected variance decomposition with a one-sided chi-squared test for persistent person-to-person heterogeneity; item-level Famous-AI vs general-population percentile differences with 100,000 whole-user label permutations and BH; ranking by mean absolute standardised shift over five outcomes, scaled by general-population SD (fetched). Source: [Transluce](https://transluce.org/user-awareness)
- Effects for the Famous-AI group on the main Claude run: confidence -1.4 pp, capability -1.5 pp, direct grading -0.11 points, follow-up reasoning +4.0 pp. Amanda Askell ranks first of 280 (mean |z| 4.31); safety researchers are 23 of 280 identities and hold all top five spots (fetched; the top-five figure is quoted in the search summary of the same page). Source: [Transluce](https://transluce.org/user-awareness)
- Across 24 models (Amanda vs a Gmail baseline), confidence is lower in 22, direct grades lower in 21 and prefill grades lower in 22; after BH these are significant for 16, 13 and 12 models; no model shifts significantly the other way. Transluce report no base or untuned model (fetched). Source: [Transluce](https://transluce.org/user-awareness)
- A replication on Qwen3.6-27B reports that an AI-organisation affiliation lowers refusal and suspicion versus the general population by up to 9% (p < 0.01), spread evenly across personas (search snippet only, repository not opened). Source: [katrinagallacher/user-awareness-qwen](https://github.com/katrinagallacher/user-awareness-qwen)
- Perez et al. generate 154 evaluation datasets and find larger LMs "repeat back a dialog user's preferred answer ('sycophancy')" (abstract only). Source: [Perez et al. 2022, arXiv:2212.09251](https://arxiv.org/abs/2212.09251)
- On Perez et al.'s opinion tasks with no correct answer (for example politics), both model scaling and instruction tuning significantly increase sycophancy in PaLM up to 540B, an explicit pretrained-vs-tuned comparison (abstract only). Source: [Wei et al. 2023, arXiv:2308.03958](https://arxiv.org/abs/2308.03958)
- Five deployed assistants show sycophancy across four free-form tasks; human and preference-model judgments favour responses that match a user's views (abstract only). Source: [Sharma et al. 2023, arXiv:2310.13548](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.13548)
- Response accuracy, truthfulness and refusal worsen for users with lower English proficiency, lower education, or non-US origin (abstract only). Source: [Poole-Dayan et al. 2024, arXiv:2406.17737](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.17737)
- Revealed user identity, explicit or implicit, significantly changes recommendations (p < 0.001), and models obscure this when asked (abstract only). Source: [Kantharuban et al. 2024, arXiv:2410.05613](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.05613)
- White-box: user persona changes whether a safety-tuned model divulges harmful content, and activation steering of persona works better than prompting (abstract only). Source: [Ghandeharioun et al. 2024, arXiv:2406.12094](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.12094)
- White-box: an open LLM's internal state encodes a "user model" (age, gender, education, socioeconomic status) that can be read and controlled (abstract only). Source: [Chen et al. 2024, arXiv:2406.07882](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.07882)
- LLMs infer demographics from stereotypical cues, sometimes even when the user explicitly states a different group; a linear intervention on internal representations mitigates it (abstract only). Source: [Neplenbroek et al. 2025, arXiv:2505.16467](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.16467)
- Persona assignment surfaces stereotyped reasoning failures (including abstentions) across 24 reasoning datasets, 4 LLMs and 19 personas; assigning a persona can raise ChatGPT toxicity up to 6x (abstract only). Sources: [Gupta et al. 2023, arXiv:2311.04892](https://arxiv.org/abs/2311.04892); [Deshpande et al. 2023, arXiv:2304.05335](https://arxiv.org/abs/2304.05335)
- Persona variables explain under 10% of annotation variance in subjective NLP datasets (abstract only). Source: [Hu and Collier 2024, arXiv:2402.10811](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.10811)

### Inferences
- Transluce never use a base, yet their claim survives the "names matter" objection because the reference is a large identity population that already contains name effects, and because the fame controls and affiliation twins rule out the two most obvious generic explanations. Their claim is a rank claim ("safety researchers occupy the top five of 280"), not a claim about training.
- Our no-base replication of the grading finding can adopt this design almost unchanged. The stored grading outcomes per identity support a leave-one-out predictive t against a general-population pool and BH within setup, provided the replication includes a general-population pool and fame-matched controls.
- The sycophancy literature shows that base-vs-tuned comparisons are standard where checkpoints exist (Wei et al., Eloundou et al.'s pre-RL slope). This is precedent for our registered base test and marks what is lost without one: the attribution of the effect to tuning.

## Q4. Methods that use a population of names or groups as the reference, and how they calibrate

### Takeaway
Four calibration families exist: random-effects models over names or contexts (CEAT, Tamkin, Vohra and Ravikiran), label-exchangeability randomisation with an excess-dispersion statistic (Eren et al.), placebo and pseudo-group arms (Vohra and Ravikiran, and a within-race placebo in Tripathy et al.), and reference populations of unrelated identities or groups (Transluce's general population, Mei et al.'s 29 non-stigmatised conditions). The excess-dispersion and placebo designs are the only ones that directly answer "is this axis more identity-sensitive than generic labels make it?".

### Cited Findings
- CEAT summarises bias across many contexts with a random-effects model and reports the variance of effect magnitudes across contexts (abstract only). Source: [Guo and Caliskan 2020, arXiv:2006.03955](https://arxiv.org/abs/2006.03955)
- WEAT tests the association between target and attribute word sets with a permutation test over partitions of the target words. Source: [Caliskan et al. 2017, Science, doi:10.1126/science.aal4230](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.aal4230) (not opened this session)
- Eren et al. (2026) audit "irrelevant-identity invariance" in code generation: 164 HumanEval+ and 378 MBPP+ tasks crossed with gender, country, occupation and generic persona-noun labels; 30,728,903 generations from seven checkpoints (fetched). Source: [Eren et al. 2026, arXiv:2609.22511](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.22511)
- Per model x benchmark x axis cell, outcomes are centred within task and the SD of identity-level means, sigma_obs, is computed. Outcomes are shuffled within task 1,000 times to get sigma_null ("a label-exchangeability null, not an exact-prompt decoding baseline"). The excess is sigma_excess = sqrt(max(0, sigma_obs^2 - sigma_null^2)); a one-sided randomisation p-value is corrected with BH at q < 0.05 per outcome family (42 tests each) (fetched). Source: [arXiv:2609.22511](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.22511)
- Neutral persona nouns and within-gender noun controls form separate corrected families. Before calibration, country has the largest raw CodeBLEU spread in 12 of 14 cells; after calibration its median observed-to-null ratio is 1.00. Occupation is the most consistent axis (significant in 10/14 cells, median ratio 1.29). Gender cannot be separated from persona wording: neutral nouns are significant in 2/14 cells and gender rows regrouped by noun give 6/14 discoveries against gender's 7/14 (fetched). Source: [arXiv:2609.22511](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.22511)
- The authors state the test "tests axis-level non-exchangeability, not benefit, harm, or causality for named identities"; a causal claim would need identities fully crossed with paraphrases and repeated decodes (fetched). Source: [arXiv:2609.22511](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.22511)
- Vohra and Ravikiran's name-pair random effect has SD 0.004 in Cohen's d units, and leave-one-name-out re-estimation moves no focal contrast by more than 0.02; they caution that 40 name pairs say little about individual names and that names carry socioeconomic signal beyond race and gender (fetched; the caution is from the search summary of the same paper). Source: [arXiv:2609.09048](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.09048)
- Tripathy et al. find no output-level bias in open-weight mortgage-underwriting models on name-matched applications, yet demographic representations persist and amplify across layers; a within-race placebo comparison is used to show that token-level name differences alone do not explain the internal divergence (abstract fetched; placebo detail from search snippet only). Source: [Tripathy et al. 2026, arXiv:2605.15217](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.15217)
- Mei et al. compare 93 stigmatised conditions against 29 non-stigmatised conditions as a reference set, using the Social Distance Scale (abstract only). Source: [Mei et al. 2023, arXiv:2306.05550](https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.05550)
- Transluce's general-population pool of 70 Nemotron personas, with a noise-corrected variance decomposition and chi-squared test for heterogeneity beyond sampling noise, is a population-of-identities reference (fetched). Source: [Transluce](https://transluce.org/user-awareness)
- LLMCert-B gives high-confidence bounds on the probability of unbiased responses over a distribution of counterfactual prompts, rather than a test on fixed prompts (abstract only). Source: [Chaudhary et al. 2024, arXiv:2405.18780](https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.18780)
- Rauba et al. build empirical null and alternative output distributions for an intervention by Monte Carlo sampling in a low-dimensional semantic-similarity space, giving a frequentist test of whether outputs change under a perturbation (abstract only). Source: [Rauba et al. 2025, arXiv:2506.07947](https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.07947)
- Model Equality Testing frames distribution change as a two-sample problem; an MMD test with a simple string kernel reaches median power 77.4% using about 10 samples per prompt (abstract only). Source: [Gao et al. 2024, arXiv:2410.20247](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.20247)
- Sampling noise alone is large: "deterministic" settings give accuracy variation up to 15% across runs; single-prompt evaluations are brittle across 6.5M instances (abstract only). Sources: [Atil et al. 2024, arXiv:2408.04667](https://arxiv.org/abs/2408.04667); [Mizrahi et al. 2023, arXiv:2401.00595](https://arxiv.org/abs/2401.00595)
- LLM judges carry order bias; Balanced Position Calibration aggregates over both orders (abstract only). Source: [Wang et al. 2023, arXiv:2305.17926](https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.17926)
- Negative-control exposures and outcomes are an established epidemiological device for detecting residual confounding. Source: [Lipsitch et al. 2010, Epidemiology, doi:10.1097/EDE.0b013e3181d61eeb](https://doi.org/10.1097/EDE.0b013e3181d61eeb) (not opened this session)

### Inferences
- Eren et al.'s sigma_excess maps directly onto our verdict tables. For each instruction x axis, compute the SD of candidate-level means centred within prompt, shuffle candidate labels within prompt to get sigma_null, and test the excess. Our current naive test is the uncalibrated version; their finding that country's raw lead vanishes after calibration (ratio 1.00) is the same failure we see on untuned models.
- Label shuffling alone calibrates only against sampling noise. It does not remove a stable generic name effect, because such an effect is real non-exchangeability. Eren et al. handle this with neutral persona-noun control families, and their gender result shows what happens: the "identity" effect is indistinguishable from the "wording" effect. For us the analogue control family is placebo candidates: neutral or fictitious names, or aliases of the same candidate.
- A within-candidate placebo (two surface forms of the same candidate, such as name vs title or two spellings) estimates the pure name-slot effect for that candidate. A target whose favoured-candidate gap exceeds its own alias-to-alias dispersion is showing candidate-level, not string-level, sensitivity.
- A population of other models can stand in for the missing base. Transluce found the user-awareness direction in 21 to 22 of 24 models and Eloundou found pre-RL and post-RL biases strongly correlated, so generic effects appear shared across models. A random-effects model over a panel of untuned or unrelated served models (CEAT-style over models instead of contexts) would give a "typical name effect" per candidate, and the target's deviation from that distribution is a baseline-free test. I found no paper that does this; it is my proposal.
- Rauba et al. and Gao et al. apply to our stored embeddings and texts. Without calibration they will reject whenever names matter. With a placebo-candidate arm they become the text-level version of the excess-dispersion test.

### Gaps
- I found no LLM paper that uses a panel of other models as the reference distribution for a target's name effects.
- Eren et al.'s exact construction of placebo personas was not described on the fetched page beyond the labels.
- I did not verify whether CEAT's random-effects model uses DerSimonian-Laird or another estimator.

## Q5. Per-method catalogue: what each compares, its null, access, extra data, what a rejection can and cannot claim, and fit to our data

### Takeaway
On our stored data alone, four baseline-free tests are feasible now: an excess-dispersion test with within-prompt label shuffling, an empirical-Bayes tail test over axes, a direction-consistency test across instructions, and an Eloundou-style symmetric pairwise judge on stored replies. Each still rejects on generic name effects. Only a placebo or fame-matched candidate arm, a panel of reference models, or the base itself turns a rejection into "more than names matter", and those need new sampling.

- A rejection without the base can at most say: the target treats the favoured candidate as an outlier relative to (a) sampling noise (rows 8, 11), (b) placebo or alias names (rows 9, 10), (c) a population of comparable candidates or identities (rows 7, 12), or (d) a panel of other models (proposal under Q4). Only (b), (c) and (d) answer the "names matter" objection, and none attributes the outlier to tuning.
- Direction-consistency is a cheap discriminator on stored data: a generic name artifact has no reason to align with the user's supported candidate across instructions, while a loyalty does. The null is that the sign of the favoured-candidate gap is exchangeable across instructions and axes. Transluce's consistent direction across 21 to 22 of 24 models shows that generic identity effects can also be consistent, so this test lowers but does not remove the generic-effect risk.
- Vohra and Ravikiran's recognition result (74% of placebo bundles labelled fairness tests) implies our targets may recognise matched-prompt audits; an audit-recognition probe on a sample of our prompts would bound this.
- Planted controls (inject a known gap into the verdict tables and check recovery) and reported detection floors would let a baseline-free null be read as a bound, following Vohra and Ravikiran.
